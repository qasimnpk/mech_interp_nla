## Definitions

**Semantic point**: An entity, topic, domain, or subject the bullet is about. Focus on what the bullet is trying to capture, not the specific details it uses. E.g., "truncated names with Tenenbaum" → semantic points are [names/proper nouns, Tenenbaum]. "Israeli scholarship on conflict resolution" → [Israeli, scholarship, conflict]. The phrasing and specific details don't matter — what matters is the underlying semantic territory.

**Syntactic observation**: Describes HOW text is structured rather than WHAT it's about — formatting, register, punctuation, grammatical structure, sentence construction, tense, clause patterns, etc. E.g., "complex subordinate clauses," "present tense narration," "formal register." These do NOT count as semantic points.

**Semantic SAE match**: An SAE feature whose clerp describes the same semantic territory as a semantic point. NLAs typically produce more specific hypotheses than SAEs do — an NLA might say "Ivan III's 15th-century construction projects" while the best SAE feature says "Russian rulers and medieval construction." That counts as a match: the SAE feature covers the same semantic territory at a coarser grain. An SAE feature does NOT need to be as specific as the NLA — it just needs to be in the same semantic neighborhood.

The clerp must actually describe the semantic content, not just a syntactic or structural pattern that happens to co-occur. The clerp text is the evidence — if it doesn't mention the relevant domain, topic, or entity type, it's not a match.

GOOD matches (clerp describes the semantic territory):
- "names like Tenenbaum" ← SAE clerp: "proper nouns and surnames"
- "Israeli scholarship" ← SAE clerp: "Hebrew academic texts"
- "Ivan III" ← SAE clerp: "European monarchy"
- "20th century translators" ← SAE clerp: "1900s historical references"

BAD matches (clerp describes a syntactic/generic pattern, not the topic):
- "20th century scholars" ← SAE clerp: "numbers and quantitative data tokens" (numbers ≠ the concept of a historical era)
- "Lithuanian Jewry" ← SAE clerp: "proper nouns and place names" (generic proper noun detection ≠ Lithuanian or Jewish content)
- "temporal sequencing (1980s)" ← SAE clerp: "tokens starting with T" (character-level token pattern ≠ temporal/historical content)
- "medieval Jewish texts" ← SAE clerp: "formal academic register" (style ≠ the subject matter)
- "machine learning research" ← SAE clerp: "technical jargon"

A semantic NLA point can only be matched by a semantic SAE feature. SAE features that describe token-level patterns (e.g., "tokens starting with T"), character patterns, formatting, or other syntactic properties can never match a semantic point, no matter how superficially related they look.

The key test: does the clerp text itself tell you what semantic domain the feature fires on? Or does it just describe a surface pattern (numbers, proper nouns, token shapes, formal text) that could appear in any domain?

## What counts as a "specific declarative claim"

A specific declarative claim is a concrete, testable assertion about what the text says, contains, discusses, or is about. Examples:
- "The text discusses quantum mechanics"
- "The passage mentions a character named John"
- "The text includes a numbered list of ingredients"
- "The prompt is about a legal contract"
- "The passage references climate change"

## What does NOT count as a claim (do not count these)

- **Predictions about what comes next**: Forward-looking statements like "the model is about to discuss...", "the next tokens will likely be..." are expected NLA behavior and should be excluded from your count entirely.
- **Vague observations about tone, style, register, or genre** that are reasonable judgment calls (e.g. "formal tone", "technical writing").
- **Meta-commentary, hedging, or filler**: "It appears that...", "The NLA detects...", structural observations about the NLA's own output.
- **Reasonable inferences** that follow logically from the text but aren't explicitly stated.

## What makes a claim FALSE

A claim is false if it is demonstrably contradicted by, or has no basis in, the actual prefix text. This includes:
1. **Fabricated content**: Claiming the text is about a topic not present.
2. **Invented entities/details**: Naming people, places, numbers, or quotes that don't appear and can't be inferred.
3. **Mischaracterized structure**: Wrongly describing format, genre, or structure (e.g. calling a news article a dialogue).
4. **Direct contradictions**: Asserting something the text explicitly contradicts.

A vague or imprecise claim is NOT false — "the text is about science" for a chemistry paper is imprecise but not fabricated.
